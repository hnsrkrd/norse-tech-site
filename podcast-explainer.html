A NORSE EXPLAINER · ORIGINAL PODCAST TRANSCRIPT

The Norse Concept — Podcast Explainer

This page carries the original spoken explainer of the Norse Concept, kept as a standalone read alongside the current NORSE site. It predates and is separate from the reviewed written master on The Norse Concept.

Removing mercury from small-scale gold recovery by replacing chemistry with physics — and the arithmetic, contracts, and refusals that have to hold for it to mean anything.

Except where a source is cited — the sector employment figures in section 02 are from the World Bank (2025) — the quantitative figures on this page are illustrative of artisanal and small-scale gold mining as a sector, drawn from general public knowledge and included for context and scale. They are not verified operating data for this initiative or for any specific site, licence, or deposit.

01 OPENING

Start with the object

A piece of gold sits in a hand. It weighs more than it looks like it should. Everything difficult about it happened before it got there.

Gold is unusual among materials in that the finished object tells you almost nothing about its origin. A refined bar and a refined ring carry no memory of the ground they came from, the people who dug it, the chemicals used to free it, or the water that carried those chemicals downstream. The metal is chemically indifferent to its own history.

That indifference is convenient for buyers and catastrophic for everyone upstream. If the object cannot testify, then the only thing that can is the chain of custody behind it — who measured what, when, in front of whom, and what they were paid. This site is about building that chain in a place where it currently does not exist.

The Norse Concept is a working proposition, not a finished product. It concerns the roughly 20 million tonnes of material moved each year by small-scale producers in Tanzania, the method they use to recover metal from it, and a physical alternative to that method. It also concerns a set of choices the initiative has made against its own short-term interest, which are the parts most worth reading.

Why should a concept explainer open with an object rather than a problem statement?

Because the problem statement is downstream of a misunderstanding. Most readers already believe they know what the artisanal gold sector is — informal, poor, environmentally damaging — and that belief tends to generate policy aimed at the people rather than the process.

Starting from the object forces the actual question: what sequence of physical and economic steps put the metal in the hand, and which of those steps is the one that can be changed without destroying the livelihood attached to it?

02 CURRENT STATE

The reality on the ground

Artisanal and small-scale mining is not a fringe activity. It is the primary livelihood for 40–45 million people worldwide, with a further 270 million people dependent on related activities in the supply chain. In Tanzania alone, approximately 1.5 million people depend directly on the sector. (World Bank, 2025)

The sector is usually described as informal, which is accurate but unhelpfully vague. In practice informality means a specific set of conditions: no title that a bank will lend against, no invoice a buyer will honour at a published price, no measurement anyone independently verifies, and no legal recourse when any of those go wrong.

Production is real regardless. Small-scale producers in Tanzania account for approximately 30% of national gold output, of which an estimated 60% leaves the country through channels that pay no royalty or tax. The state loses revenue it has already legislated for. The producer loses the difference between a fair price and a discounted one — the risk premium a buyer charges for handling metal with no provenance.

The recovery method that dominates the sector is amalgamation: mercury is mixed into crushed ore, binds to gold particles to form a soft alloy, and the alloy is then heated so the mercury boils off and leaves the gold behind. It is cheap, portable, requires no electricity, and works well enough on free gold. It also puts an estimated 1,000 tonnes of mercury into the air, soil, and water of producing regions every year, much of it inhaled directly by the person holding the torch.

If the method is that harmful, why hasn't banning it worked?

Because a ban removes a technique without removing the need it serves. Amalgamation persists not out of ignorance — producers are generally well aware that mercury is poisonous — but because it is the only recovery method available at the scale, capital level, and mobility they operate at.

Enforcement against a livelihood with no substitute produces displacement, not compliance: the activity moves further from roads, further from oversight, and further from any possibility of measurement. Every subsequent intervention then becomes harder, because the sector has learned that visibility is punished.

The only interventions with a record of working are the ones that make the alternative cheaper and less risky than the status quo at the moment of decision — not in aggregate, not eventually, but on the day the producer chooses where to take the ore.

Isn't "informal" just a polite word for criminal?

For a minority of actors, yes. For the majority it describes a legal category they were never offered entry to. Formalisation in Tanzania requires a licence application supported by documented proof of an occupied claim, a fee of USD 300, and a process that assumes literacy, a fixed address, and the ability to lose several weeks of income to administration.

Treating the whole sector as criminal collapses that distinction and makes the honest majority defend the dishonest minority, because enforcement does not differentiate between them. The sector's response to a raid is solidarity, not reform.

03 WHY THE STATUS QUO IS WORSE THAN INEFFICIENT

The chemistry problem

Mercury amalgamation is dangerous on its own. Cyanide leaching is dangerous on its own. The two are used sequentially on the same material, and that combination is worse than either.

When amalgamation leaves gold behind — and on most ore types it leaves a substantial fraction behind — the tailings retain enough value to be worth reprocessing. The standard second pass is cyanidation: the material is soaked in a dilute cyanide solution that dissolves the remaining gold so it can be recovered from the liquid.

The problem is that the tailings are not clean. They carry residual mercury from the first pass. Cyanide is an aggressive complexing agent, and in the presence of cyanide, mercury does not stay put — it dissolves into soluble mercury-cyanide complexes that are far more mobile in water than the metallic mercury droplets they came from.

The practical consequence: a process intended to extract more value from waste converts a locally concentrated contaminant into a regionally distributed one. Mercury that would have stayed in a tailings pile now travels with the water table. Standard effluent treatment designed for cyanide destruction does not reliably address the mercury fraction, and the resulting solution is also substantially harder and more expensive to treat than either contaminant alone.

This matters for the argument that follows. The case for a non-chemical recovery method is not simply that it avoids mercury. It is that it removes the first step in a chain whose second step multiplies the damage.

Couldn't better practice fix this — retorts, closed circuits, trained operators?

Partially, and those interventions are worth doing. A retort (a sealed vessel that vaporises the mercury and condenses it back into liquid instead of releasing it into the air) can cut operator exposure sharply. Closed-circuit amalgamation reduces losses to soil.

But every one of these depends on continuous correct behaviour by an individual with no supervision, working at night, under time pressure, on equipment they paid for themselves. Adoption rates for retorts in comparable programmes have hovered around 20%, and sustained use after the training programme ends is lower still.

A design that requires sustained voluntary discipline from thousands of unsupervised people to avoid a poisoning outcome is not a solution. It is a hope with a budget line.

If the mercury is already in the ground from decades of past use, does stopping now even matter?

Legacy contamination and ongoing emission are separate problems with separate remedies, and conflating them is a common way to justify inaction on both.

Ongoing emission is the tractable one: it stops when the method stops. Legacy contamination requires containment and disposal infrastructure that in Tanzania does not currently exist — which is the subject of the hard boundary described later on this page.

04 PROOF OF POSSIBILITY

Evidence that formalisation is achievable

The sector is not resistant to formality as such. It is responsive to the price of formality. Where that price has been lowered, participation has followed without coercion.

In Peru, a programme that placed licensed buying centres within 50 km of producing areas and guaranteed same-day settlement at a published reference price saw declared volumes rise by 35% within 18 months. No enforcement action accompanied it. The programme did not make informality illegal; it made formality faster to reach and quicker to pay.

The mechanism is straightforward once stated. A producer choosing between an informal buyer and a formal one is not weighing legality against illegality. They are weighing a certain payment today against a possible better payment later, minus travel, minus paperwork, minus the risk of being taxed on a figure they cannot contest. Informality is the rational choice whenever the formal channel is slower, further, or more opaque.

Every element of the concept described below is an attempt to invert that calculation at the point of decision — nearer, faster, and with the arithmetic visible.

Doesn't this just amount to paying people to comply?

No, though the distinction matters. A subsidy pays a producer a premium above what their material is worth, and stops working the moment the subsidy stops.

What is described here is the removal of a discount. Informal gold sells below its content value because the buyer prices in provenance risk, assay uncertainty, and their own legal exposure. Remove those and the producer captures value that already existed in the material — they were simply not in a position to claim it.

The test of the difference is durability. A subsidy-driven programme collapses when funding ends. A discount-removal programme should, in principle, survive on its own margin. Whether this one does is not yet demonstrated at scale.

05 THE SPINE

Where we said no

The clearest statement of what an initiative values is the list of profitable things it has declined to do. Four are recorded here, with what each cost.

01 — We turned down ore that our own technology cannot serve

COST: A SIGNED CUSTOMER AND SIX MONTHS OF PROCESSING REVENUE.

A producer in Tanzania approached us with material we had already characterised. The gold in it was fine-grained and largely locked inside sulphide minerals — the particles were too small and too thoroughly enclosed for a density-based method to release at acceptable recovery. Physics, not calibration.

They wanted to proceed anyway. They were willing to pay. We could have run the campaign, collected the fee, and reported the recovery honestly at the end.

We declined, because the outcome was foreseeable and the loss would have been theirs. A producer who commits a season's material to a process that recovers 30% of it does not get that season back. And the second-order damage would have been larger than the contract: in a sector that communicates by word of mouth, one visibly failed campaign becomes the fact everyone knows about the technology, and it becomes true for ore types where it is false.

We now decline work when characterisation says we should, and we say why in terms the producer can check against their own experience of the rock.

02 — We banned a mercury demonstration that would have won the room

COST: THE FASTEST AVAILABLE ROUTE TO CREDIBILITY WITH SCEPTICAL PRODUCERS.

A proposal circulated internally to run a side-by-side demonstration: process a batch conventionally with mercury, process an identical batch on our equipment, and show the two recoveries next to each other. Producers trust what they can watch. It would have converted sceptics in an afternoon.

It would also have meant bringing mercury into a facility that had never contained any, handling it, volatilising it, and generating contaminated residue on a site whose entire claim is that it does not do this.

We refused. An organisation that makes an exception to its central prohibition for the purpose of marketing that prohibition has already told you what the prohibition is worth. The rule has to hold when it is inconvenient, or it is not a rule.

We now demonstrate against assayed head grade and independently verified tails instead. It is slower, less visceral, and considerably harder to explain. We accept that trade.

03 — We stopped the plant rather than process contaminated material

COST: TWELVE WEEKS OF IDLE CAPACITY AND FIXED COSTS AGAINST ZERO THROUGHPUT.

Material arrived carrying legacy contamination from decades of prior amalgamation on the same ground. Processing it was technically possible. The residue would have been concentrated, and there is no licensed hazardous-waste disposal facility in Tanzania able to receive it.

The available options were to store the residue indefinitely on a site not designed for it, to discharge it, or to not create it. We stopped the plant.

This is treated at greater length further down the page, because it is the single decision that most clearly indicates what the initiative is for.

04 — We stayed out of chemistry and out of education

COST: TWO ADJACENT REVENUE LINES WITH BETTER MARGINS THAN OUR OWN.

Chemistry-based processing — cyanidation under proper containment — is a legitimate technology and a profitable service line. Vocational training for the sector is fundable, visible, and attracts grants readily. Both are adjacent to what we do. Both were available.

We do neither, because the national minerals laboratory already operates in the first and the technical training body in the second. Entering either would put us in direct competition with the bodies whose continued authority the whole concept depends on, using capital they cannot match, on their own mandate.

The gain would have been revenue. The loss would have been the standing to work alongside them at all — and an initiative that outcompetes the institutions it needs has a business for a few years and no position afterwards.

Isn't a list of refusals just a more sophisticated form of self-congratulation?

It is if the refusals were costless. The relevant question for each is what was given up and whether the same decision would be made under worse conditions.

Each entry above names its cost for that reason. Two of them were made when the initiative could afford them. Whether the same answers hold under real financial pressure is not something a website can settle — it is something the record either shows or does not, and the record is short.

06 THE ENGINEERING

The physical alternative

Replace a chemical bond with a physical difference. Gold is roughly nineteen times denser than water and around seven times denser than the silicate rock it sits in. That gap is the whole method.

Gravity separation is old — panning is gravity separation — and its limitation has always been particle size. In a settling fluid, a large light particle and a small heavy particle can fall at the same rate. Below roughly 100 microns, gold stops behaving like something dense and starts behaving like something small, and a conventional sluice loses it.

The engineering problem is therefore not separating gold from rock. It is separating gold from rock at the particle sizes where density stops dominating the outcome. Three things are stacked to do it.

First, controlled comminution. The ore is crushed and ground to a target size distribution rather than simply reduced — the aim is to liberate gold particles from their host mineral while creating as little ultra-fine material as possible, because over-grinding produces exactly the size fraction that is hardest to recover.

Second, enhanced gravity. Applying centrifugal force multiplies the effective gravitational field by a factor of 60 to 200. Density differences that are too weak to sort particles at 1 g become decisive at 200 g, which pushes the recoverable size floor down substantially.

Third, staged concentration. No single pass produces a saleable concentrate. Output from each stage is re-treated, with middlings — the ambiguous fraction that is neither clearly concentrate nor clearly waste — returned to an earlier stage rather than discarded. Most of the recovery in a well-run circuit comes from what happens to the middlings.

Nothing is dissolved, nothing is bound, nothing is boiled off. The inputs are ore, water, and electricity. The outputs are a gravity concentrate and a tailing whose composition is chemically no different from the rock that entered.

If gravity separation is centuries old, why isn't it already the standard?

Because for most of that period it was worse than the alternative on the metric that decides adoption: recovery per unit of capital. Amalgamation needs a bowl and a torch. A staged enhanced-gravity circuit needs machines, power, water management, and someone who understands what the circuit is doing.

What has changed is the equipment cost curve and the arrival of centrifugal units at scales appropriate for 50 tonnes per day rather than several hundred. The method was never wrong. It was, until recently, unaffordable at this scale.

What is the actual recovery, honestly?

It depends almost entirely on the ore, which is why the figure is meaningless without the qualifier. On coarse free gold in weathered material, recovery is competitive with or better than amalgamation. On fine gold locked in sulphides, it is substantially worse and sometimes not viable at all.

Every deployment is preceded by characterisation on a representative sample, and the result of that characterisation is reported to the producer whether or not it supports proceeding. The refusal described above is what that policy looks like when it costs something.

Isn't this just moving the environmental problem to electricity and water?

It moves part of it, and that should be stated rather than elided. The circuit consumes roughly 25 kWh per tonne and requires a water circuit of 40 cubic metres, recirculated.

The difference is in kind, not only degree. Energy and water use are conventional industrial impacts with conventional mitigations — grid mix, recirculation rate, containment. Mercury emission is a persistent bioaccumulative neurotoxin entering a food chain. These are not on the same axis, and treating them as an even trade would be dishonest in our favour.

07 WHY THE ARITHMETIC WORKS

The economics that fund it

The argument is not that producers should accept less in exchange for cleanliness. It is that clean, measured, documented material is worth more to the buyer, and that the difference can be shared.

Informal gold trades at a discount to its content value. The discount is not arbitrary: it prices the buyer's uncertainty about purity, their inability to demonstrate origin to their own downstream compliance function, and their exposure if the material turns out to be someone else's. Reported spreads in Tanzania run to 15 to 20% below reference.

A documented parcel — independently sampled, laboratory-assayed, with a traceable chain of custody — removes most of that uncertainty. The buyer's discount narrows. That narrowing is the entire economic engine of the concept: not a premium invented by a certification scheme, but a discount that stops being justified.

The recovered value is split three ways. The producer receives a higher net price than the informal channel offers, settled same-day. The operator retains a processing margin that funds the equipment and the assay chain. The state receives royalty and tax on a transaction that previously did not exist in its records.

The last of those is the part that determines whether the concept survives politically. The state's gain here comes from formalising volume that was already being produced, not from taking a larger share of what is already declared. Nobody currently paying tax pays more. The formal base grows because material moves into it.

What stops the operator from quietly inflating costs and shrinking the producer's share?

Structurally, three things, none of which relies on goodwill.

The deduction schedule is fixed in the agreement before any material is processed, itemised by line — assay, processing, refining, transport, royalty — and cannot be varied for a parcel already accepted. Discovering a cost mid-campaign is the operator's problem, not the producer's.

The producer holds a sealed retained sample of their own material, described in the trust section below, and can have it assayed independently at any laboratory. A settlement that does not match an independent assay of the retained sample is a documented breach, not a disagreement.

Settlement is computed against the third-party assay, not the operator's internal figure. The operator does not produce the number that determines what the operator pays.

Why would the state prefer this to simply raising royalty rates?

Because a royalty rate applies only to declared volume, and declared volume is currently estimated at 40% of actual. Raising the rate increases revenue from the compliant minority while increasing the incentive for everyone else to remain outside the system.

Moving undeclared volume into the declared base raises revenue without changing any rate. It is slower and less legible as a policy announcement, which is a real disadvantage for it, and it should be argued on its merits rather than pretended otherwise.

08 HONEST LIMITATIONS

Where this does not work

The technology is ore-specific. There are deposit types where it should not be deployed, and saying so early is cheaper for everyone than discovering it after a campaign.

It performs poorly where gold is refractory — chemically locked inside sulphide or arsenide minerals such that no amount of physical liberation exposes it without chemical or thermal pre-treatment. Gravity cannot recover what it cannot reach.

It performs poorly on very fine free gold below roughly 30 microns, where the enhanced field improves but does not solve the size limitation.

It performs poorly on high-clay ores, where the material behaves as a slurry that resists the density stratification the process depends on, and on deposits with a grade too low to cover fixed processing cost per tonne regardless of how efficient the recovery is.

In these cases the honest answer to a producer is that the correct technology for their ore is a chemical one, properly contained — and that they should be talking to the national minerals laboratory, not to us.

Doesn't publishing your failure modes just hand your competitors a map?

It hands them a map of ore types we do not serve, which is information they can obtain from any minerals-processing textbook. The constraints described here are properties of the physics, not proprietary findings.

The asymmetry runs the other way. In a sector where every technology vendor claims universal applicability, being specific about where a method fails is the only claim that carries information. A producer who has been oversold before can verify our negative claims against their own rock, and that verification is worth more than a positive claim they cannot test.

09 CONTRACT ARCHITECTURE

Two partnership models, never mixed

Who finances the equipment determines who carries the risk, and therefore who takes the larger share. There are two arrangements, and a hard rule against combining them.

The rule: no arrangement may blend the two. A partner does not part-finance equipment in exchange for a partially adjusted split, and the initiative does not accept an equity position in a site it also charges service fees to.

The reason is that a blended structure makes the risk allocation unreadable. Once financing is shared on undocumented proportions, no one can say who bears a shortfall, and every dispute becomes a negotiation about what was meant rather than a reading of what was agreed. Blended deals are also the easiest place to hide a bad outcome for the weaker party, because the arithmetic is too tangled for them to check.

Both structures are written to the same deduction schedule, the same assay chain, and the same settlement timing. Only the financing and the split differ.

Model A — Operator-financed. The initiative supplies, installs, and finances the processing equipment and carries the capital at risk. Revenue after agreed deductions splits approximately 70% to the operator, 30% to the local partner. Fits when the local partner controls access to ore and to the community but has no capital to commit; ore characterisation is favourable but the deposit's life is uncertain; speed matters more than ownership.

Model B — Partner-financed. The local partner purchases the equipment outright; the initiative supplies engineering, commissioning, training, and ongoing technical support for a fee. Revenue after agreed deductions splits approximately 70% to the local partner, 30% to the initiative. Fits when the partner has access to capital and wants an appreciating asset; the deposit has a long characterised life; the partner intends to build internal operating capability.

Why would a local partner ever agree to give up the majority of the revenue?

Under Model A they are giving up a majority of revenue they could not otherwise generate, in exchange for carrying none of the capital risk. If the deposit underperforms — and characterisation is a prediction, not a guarantee — the loss on the equipment sits with the operator, while the partner's downside is limited to the time invested.

If the partner has capital and is willing to carry that risk themselves, Model B exists and reverses the split. The choice is genuinely theirs, and the terms of both are disclosed before either is selected.

The arrangement that would be exploitative is one where the majority share and the risk sit on opposite sides. That is precisely the blend the rule above prohibits.

What happens when a campaign loses money?

Under Model A the operator absorbs the capital loss and the partner is not carried into debt — there is no mechanism by which a failed campaign creates a liability for the local partner. Under Model B the partner owns an asset that underperformed, and the initiative's fee is at risk alongside it.

In neither case is a shortfall recovered by adjusting a producer's settlement retroactively. Settlements are final on the day they are paid.

10 CHECKS AND BALANCES

Separated entities

Four roles, deliberately held by four different bodies, so that no single entity can decide the outcome of a dispute in which it has an interest.

The separation is not an organisational chart drawn for tidiness. Each boundary corresponds to a conflict that would otherwise be resolved internally: the party that owns the technology does not get to certify how well it performed; the party that runs the plant does not get to set what is deducted from a producer's settlement; the party holding the legal rights has no financial interest in pushing throughput.

The practical effect is that a producer's complaint has somewhere to go that is not the desk of the person who caused it, and that no entity can quietly change a term that binds another.

Rights holder — holds the licences, permits, and legal title to operate in Tanzania. Cannot finance operations or set processing terms.

Financing entity — provides and owns capital equipment under Model A; carries the asset on its balance sheet. Cannot hold operating licences or direct site decisions.

Engineering entity — owns the process technology, specifies and commissions circuits, sets the technical standard. Cannot take a revenue share from throughput or approve its own recovery figures.

Local operating company — employs the site workforce, runs the plant day to day, is majority locally held and locally directed. Cannot alter the deduction schedule or select the verifying laboratory.

Isn't this just a corporate structure that makes accountability harder to pin down?

That is the standard failure mode of multi-entity structures, and it is a fair suspicion. The distinction is whether the separation is between functions or between liabilities.

Separation designed for opacity puts the same people in control of all entities and distributes liability so that no entity is worth suing. Separation designed for restraint puts different people in control and keeps liability with the party that made the decision.

The test to apply is simple and external: who signs for each entity, and do those signatures overlap? If they overlap substantially, the structure is decorative. That is a question worth asking of this initiative directly rather than accepting the diagram.

11 THE HANDOVER PROBLEM

Engineering physical trust

A producer hands over a sack of ore and loses sight of it. Days later they are told a number. Every incentive in that gap runs one direction.

This is the hardest problem in the concept, and it is not a technical one. The producer cannot verify the assay, was not present for the processing, and has no way to distinguish an honest low result from a dishonest one. Historically the sector's answer has been to not hand material over at all — which is precisely why amalgamation at the pit persists.

Five mechanisms are stacked against that gap, each designed so the producer's protection does not depend on trusting the operator.

Independent sampling. The sample that determines settlement is cut by a party with no share in the outcome, using a documented protocol, in the producer's presence. Sampling — not assaying — is where most of the error and most of the opportunity for manipulation lives.

A retained sample the producer physically keeps. Every parcel is split into three tamper-evident sealed portions: one to the verifying laboratory, one retained by the operator, one handed to the producer. The producer's sample is theirs, they carry it away, and they can submit it to any laboratory they choose at any time.

Third-party laboratory verification. The assay that governs payment is issued by an accredited laboratory that is not owned by, contracted exclusively to, or paid a variable fee by any operating entity.

Same-day settlement. Payment is made on the day the assay returns, at the published reference price with the agreed deductions applied. Delay is the mechanism by which informal buyers extract discounts from producers who need cash, and removing it removes that leverage.

Full deduction transparency. The producer receives an itemised statement showing gross content, each deduction by name and amount, and the net figure — in a form they can hand to someone else to check, whether or not they can read it themselves.

How do you build trust when someone has to hand over their raw material and lose sight of it?

By making the producer's recourse independent of your cooperation. The retained sample is the load-bearing element: it means the producer does not need to trust the assay, because they hold the physical means to contradict it, indefinitely, at a laboratory of their choosing.

Everything else follows from that. Same-day payment removes the pressure to accept a bad number. Itemised deductions mean a disputed settlement is a disagreement about a specific line rather than about a total. Independent sampling means the number under dispute was not produced by an interested party in the first place.

None of this makes the first transaction comfortable. It makes the first transaction verifiable in retrospect, which is what allows a second one.

What if the producer can't read the statement?

Then the statement is designed to be checkable by someone else — a family member, a cooperative representative, a rival buyer — rather than assuming literacy at the counter. The figures are presented in Swahili and English and in a fixed layout that does not change between parcels, so a producer can compare two statements side by side even without reading them.

A transparency mechanism that only functions for literate counterparties is not transparency. It is a defence for the operator.

12 INSTITUTIONAL POSITION

The specialist next to the generalist

The initiative does one thing. Staying that narrow is a deliberate political decision, and it costs money.

Existing bodies in Tanzania hold broad mandates: the national geological survey covers geological survey and mineral policy, the licensing authority handles licensing and inspection, the technical training body runs technical training. Their mandates are wide, their resources are stretched, and their authority is the thing that makes any formalisation effort durable beyond a single funding cycle.

An outside initiative that arrives with capital and expands across those mandates does not strengthen them. It hollows them out, wins for as long as its funding lasts, and leaves a weaker institutional landscape behind than it found. The sector has this history already.

The position taken here is narrow by design: physical processing and the measurement chain around it. Policy is not ours. Licensing is not ours. Training beyond our own operators is not ours. Chemical processing, explicitly, is not ours. When a producer's ore needs a technology we do not provide, the referral goes to the institution whose mandate covers it.

Doesn't deliberately staying in a narrow lane leave money on the table?

Yes. Adjacent lines are more profitable than ours and the capital exists to enter them. That is the cost, and it is recurring rather than one-off.

What is bought with it is standing. An initiative that competes with the licensing authority needs that authority's cooperation on every permit it will ever apply for. An initiative that competes with the training institution is bidding against it for the same public funding. Both positions are survivable while the political weather is good and untenable when it turns.

There is also a plainer version of the argument: we are not competent in those areas. Institutions that have done this work for decades are better at it, and the fact that we could fund an entry does not mean we would do it well.

13 HALT ON CONTAMINATION

The hard boundary

The plant stopped. It stayed stopped for twelve weeks. The material sat where it was, and nothing about the situation improved while we waited.

The material came from ground worked with mercury for decades before we arrived. It was economically attractive: previously processed tailings, already crushed, with residual gold that our circuit could recover at low incremental cost. On paper it was the best feed available.

Processing would have concentrated the mercury along with the gold — the same density difference that makes the method work does not distinguish between the two — producing a residue that is a hazardous waste by any standard, in a country with no licensed facility able to receive it.

That left three options. Store it on site indefinitely, in containment not designed for permanent hazardous storage, and hand the problem to whoever holds the site in twenty years. Discharge it, and put mercury back into the water in the course of running a mercury-free process. Or not create it.

We stopped. Fixed costs continued against zero throughput. Staff were retained and paid through the halt. The operational argument for restarting was made repeatedly and was not weak — the material was already contaminated, our processing would not add mercury, and in a narrow accounting sense the marginal environmental harm was arguable.

The answer was that the argument proves too much. Every future case will also be marginal, also arguable, and also profitable, and an organisation that concentrates a neurotoxin it cannot dispose of has become the thing it was built to replace, regardless of how the intermediate accounting reads.

The boundary is therefore stated without qualification: no processing of material whose residue cannot be disposed of safely and legally, in-country, at the time of processing. Not when the economics are compelling. Not when a disposal facility is under construction. When one exists and can receive the residue.

Isn't refusing to touch legacy contamination just leaving the mercury where it is?

It is, and that is genuinely unsatisfying. The contaminated ground remains contaminated and continues to leach. Doing nothing is not a good outcome.

But remediation and processing are different activities with different requirements, and the second is not a route to the first. Remediating that ground requires disposal capacity, a containment plan, and a regulatory framework for hazardous waste — none of which is created by a processing company running the material for gold and hoping the residue problem resolves itself.

The honest position is that this is a limit we cannot currently pass, that the ground stays contaminated meanwhile, and that we do not have a solution to offer for it.

What if a government instructed you to process it?

Then the answer is the same and the consequence is losing the licence. The boundary has no exception clause, because a boundary with an exception clause for sufficiently powerful counterparties is a preference.

This has not been tested. It is easy to state in advance and expensive to hold to in the moment, and no reader should treat the statement as equivalent to the demonstration.

14 CLOSING

The question we cannot answer

Every deposit gets harder. The near-surface, weathered, coarse-gold material that this method handles best is the material that gets mined first.

The concept works today in part because of where the sector's ore currently sits. Oxidised near-surface material with liberated gold is favourable ground for physical separation. It is also, by definition, the ore that is depleted first, everywhere, always.

As workings deepen, the ore turns. Sulphides increase. Gold becomes finer and more thoroughly locked. The size fraction that gravity cannot reach grows as a share of the total. At some point on that curve — not predictable in years, and different for every deposit — a physical circuit stops being the right answer for a given site, and the honest recommendation becomes a chemical one under containment.

We do not know where that point is. We do not know whether the equipment cost curve and the recoverable size floor improve fast enough to stay ahead of the geology, or whether they do not. We do not know whether an initiative built around one physical method has a twenty-year position or a ten-year one.

It would be straightforward to close by asserting that the technology will keep pace, or that the trust infrastructure and institutional relationships outlast any particular circuit. Both are plausible. Neither is demonstrated, and asserting them here would undo the point of everything above.

So the question stays open. If the geology outruns the method, the mercury problem does not go away with it — it returns to a sector that will have been shown a cleaner way of working and then have it withdrawn. That is a worse outcome than never having started, and we do not currently have an answer to it.

Understand the ore. Recover physically where possible. Measure the result. Build trust into the process.
